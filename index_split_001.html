Preface

The Tarot was largely a dead topic until two members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn released books and decks in the early 20th century.

A.E. Waite published his deck and book The Key to the Tarot in 1909, but his deck was the first to include the meaningful scenes for the pip cards. Other decks had some, but not with that system behind them.

Aleister Crowley published in 1944 and 1945, but made numerous modifications to cards, their names, and meanings under the prompting of the artist Lady Frieda Harris, who encouraged him to incorporate his full magical system into them.

Most modern decks owe some heritage to this Rider-Waite-Smith deck (RWS), or to the Thoth deck.

However, both drew upon a Golden Dawn internal document called Liber-T, aka Book-T, written by Macgregor Mathers (mostly) sometime between 1887 and 1897. Unfortunately Book-T only covers the pip cards.

Unfortunately, Waite’s book The Key to the Tarot raises more questions than it answers. Paraphrasing Waite, he says that the art of fortune telling is a relatively low art, and yet the Tarot contains symbolism from a secret, higher art, known to various secret societies over time. He relates it to Alchemy, Rosicrucianism, Freemasonry and Kabbalah, but he refuses to divulge any deeper secrets. He further says that (as of 1910) there are no documents that reveal these deeper secrets, and that only about the shallowest third of the knowledge is disclosed in print. He says that those who know the secrets will not reveal them, and so implies that neither will he reveal them.

How then is one to gain a proper insight into the meaning of the Tarot? First, we must gather the clues that are available to us.
