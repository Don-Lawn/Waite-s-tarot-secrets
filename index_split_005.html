The following evolution is provided by the Wikipedia article for Tree of life (Qabalah):

“The first historical instance of the modern tree of life was designed by Johann Reuchlin. Paolo Riccio's son, Hyeronomius, had actively exchanged letters and shared his father's work with Reuchlin before publication. Thus, in the year 1516, Reuchlin's diagram came to appear on the cover of Paolo Riccio's Latin translation of Joseph Gikatilla's Gates of Light. The diagram only had 17 paths and, at the time, the concepts of 10 spheres and 22 letters were still distinct in the literature. In 1573, a version sketched by Franciscus Zillettus appeared in Cesare Evoli, De divinis attributis. This version introduced several innovations that would reappear in later versions: all the spheres were of the same size, the lines became wide paths, the spheres were aligned into 3 distinct columns, Malkuth was connected to three spheres, and astrological symbols for the known celestial bodies were used in conjunction with the Hebrew names to label the spheres. However, it also had only 17 paths, albeit distributed differently. Reuchlin's version was reprinted in Johann Pistorius' compilation of 1587. Finally, several versions from unknown artists introducing 21 and 22 paths appeared in the posthumous print editions of Moses Cordovero's Pardes Rimonim between 1592 and 1609.] However, the diagrams with 22 paths lacked consistency with each other and none of them had the 22 letters. Between 1652 and 1654, Athanasius Kircher published his own version of the tree in Oedipus Aegyptiacus. Kircher might have designed his diagram in a syncretic attempt to reconcile several distinct ideas.”

Tarot was first associated with the Tree in 1781, in Antoine Court de Gébelin's multi-volume book Monde Primitif, which included essays on Tarot by himself and Le Comte de Mellet.

There is also a problem in terms of interpretation. The Pymander, and hence the majors, is clearly composed of three major parts:

  • Descent of the soul
  • Mortal life
  • Ascent of the soul

Any mapping of the majors to the tree should show some evidence of this. Instead, the normal attribution is simply a correspondence of the major arcana numbering to the path numbers, resulting in only a descent. Hermetics often talk about both descent and ascent, and yet the tarot assignment remains descent only. A better set of attributions is needed.

Twenty-six Majors?

According to both the Pymander and the Waite/Smith Tarot, the four Aces belong in the Majors. The Aces should be included in Major Arcana's depiction of the Pymander because it clearly describes the Waite cloud that carries the symbols. The total number of Majors would then be twenty-six, which corresponds better to our alphabet than to Hebrew.

Regarding Waite

Another source of concern is deliberate attempts to conceal the meaning from those who do not have the Tarot Key. Waite boasts arrogantly in his book of the same name that without the key, the unenlightened cannot hope to understand the Tarot. He then proceeds to skirt the truth for the rest of the book, describing cards and dropping hints but never directly mentioning the Pymander.

Regarding Crowley

The Crowley Thoth deck, like Waite's, is based on Mathers' Golden Dawn Book-T, but his Majors appear to be distinct from the other decks. That deck is so unique in the Tarot world, and so well detailed by Crowley and Mathers, that its quirks will not be addressed in this book. A nod to Book-T will benefit both the Waite and Crowley decks. Lady Freda Harris also encouraged Crowley to incorporate his entire magical system into his deck, resulting in a more comprehensive mapping of Tarot Majors to astrology. The Pymander technique, in particular, covers both aspects of the seven Governors for 14 cards, whereas the Crowley method covers all twenty-two cards with zodiac mapping. This work does not address these distinctions.

Astrology

We will discover as we investigate the Pymander that it contains a creation myth that incorporates elements of both Genesis and Renaissance Astrology. Fourteen of the cards correspond directly to the traditional seven heavenly bodies, representing both their primary and negative aspects. The imagery has been obscured, however, by the use of indirect mythology references. Images on cards are frequently associated with mythologies other than the primary ones. For example, the image of a different messenger, Iris, replaces one of Mercury's cards. The children of the primary gods, rather than the gods themselves, represent some of the negative aspects. As a result, the meaning of the images is obscured, making the Pymander's accidental discovery less likely.

The RWS deck was the first to use explanatory scenes on its pip cards. These scenes were created by Pamela Coleman-Smith to reflect Waite's astrological system and knowledge. This book will cover that astrology and how Pamela used it to create the cards.

Background

The de Medici family was bankers in Italy during the 1400s Renaissance. They were widely regarded as Europe's primary bankers, and they became extremely wealthy as a result of European governments' repayments of loans made to fund the Second Crusade. They were not, however, miserly, and their monetary contributions to their community were largely responsible for the art world's Renaissance period.

They sponsored a Library and offered to buy manuscripts of interest from anyone at attractive prices as part of their philanthropy. One manuscript they bought had to wait until Marsilio Ficino translated it into Latin in 1471. This was the Pymander, also known as the Divine Pymander of Hermes.

Italy was, of course, home to the Pope and so Christianity in general, although it was at about this time that the Church of England and the Lutheran church split away. The Pymander offered an alternative, or perhaps an extension to, or clarification of, the Genesis story. It was accepted by the church and Pope as a marvellous and highly significant find, and continued to be treated as such by a series of fourteen Popes, up until Clement VIII who declared it to be heretical, and burned at the stake Giordano Bruno for his refusal to denounce it, in 1600.

The story is more complicated, because the Renaissance saw a surge in mystical activity, including a revival of Neo-Platonism and Gnosticism, as well as a safe haven for the last persecuted Cathars. Many refugees from Turkey found a haven in Italy. These factors combined to spark a surge in interest in what is now known as Renaissance Magic. To avoid the Roman Catholic Church's wrath, this magic was based on both Christian and Pymander philosophy, and it was careful to invoke only spirits compatible with the philosophy of the time.

Many of the Popes who accepted the Pymander were members of the infamous Borgia family, whereas Clement was not.

Initially thought to be of great antiquity, possibly older than Genesis itself, linguistic analysis implied that it was a Gnostic Christian document from the 200s A.D. The Pymander's interpretation of the Demiurge, on the other hand, differs greatly from that of the Gnostics, identifying it as neo-platonic.

However, there are some chronological issues. Before the translation of the Pymander, the Major Arcana cards appear to have settled on the standard twenty-two, but not before the establishment of a Neoplatonist school in Italy, so the possibility of neo-platonic influence on card design remains.

The book's thesis is that the Tarot was most likely influenced by neo-platonic thought, so that when it was translated, it corresponded to the Pymander. However, due to occultists' usual secrecy and to avoid offending the church too much, a measure of secrecy must have been incorporated into the meaning of the cards. This would have reached a climax when Clement declared the Pymander heretical and drove it underground. As a result, the Tarot major arcana adopted images that disguised the Pymander as Roman mythology and Christian concepts, allowing it to avoid detection by the Church. This book traces the major arcana back to the Pymander as a mechanism for returning the cards' possible interpretations to their original meanings.

For a comprehensive history and analysis of the times, see the book Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition by the Renaissance historian Frances Yates.
