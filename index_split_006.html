Table 1 – Renaissance Timeline

Pre-1425      Various card decks are created and used for gaming

Post 1425      Duke Filippo Maria Visconti of Milan commissioned an allegorical card game based on virtues and temptations. Each suit had four extra trump cards. This comparison of Virtues and Temptations corresponds well with Pymander and later decks, as well as some Platonic virtues, and shows that Renaissance philosophy was following Pymander lines. This deck, however, lacks the more standardised format.

1438-1445      Cosimo de Medici and his intellectual circle met the Neoplatonic philosopher George Gemistos Plethan during the Council of Ferrara-Florence sessions in Florence, whose discourses on Plato and the Alexandrian mystics so captivated the learned society of Florence that they dubbed him "the second Plato."

≈1462      The Florence Platonic Academy is established, funded by Cosimo de Medici and led by Ficino.

1465      Count Boiardo of Ferrara designed a tarot deck with a poem for each card. This could be taken negatively, implying that no consistent meaning for the Tarot had yet been established, and that Trionfi was still regarded as a game in which certain cards trumped others. (Much like the modern equivalent of Magic the Gathering).

1470      The Sermones de Ludo Cum Aliis, written by a priest preaching against dice and card playing, is the earliest possible date for the Steele Sermon. He listed the trumps in the same order as they are now.

1491      Ferrara. The copper engraved Sola Busca deck was created, with its unique designs and illustrated pip cards. Each of these cards is a reference to a historical warrior, which distinguishes this deck from the standard Major Arcana. As such, it appears to be an extreme deviation, but Pamela Coleman-Rider Smith's Waite Smith deck was influenced by its minor arcana (RWS).

1490s       France conquers Milan and parts of Italy. Card production is shifting to France.

1492      Ficino finishes the Latin translation of the Pymander, but in Italy rather than France, the new tarot home.

1494      France invades Italy

≈1500      Tarocchi first appeared in Ferrara and Avignon. Trionfi is now the name of another card game played with a standard pack of cards.

1557      Lyons. Catelin Geoffroy's deck. "These are the earliest Major Arcana cards numbered in the sequence that is popular today," says Kaplan of this deck, and it "is the earliest set of tarot cards containing a numbered sequence for the Major Arcana." The Steele Sermon list is unmistakably different, both in sequence and in the actual set of cards, with The Arrow of Light, Angel, Journey, Hunchback, and so on.

≈1599      Pope Clement declares the Pymander heretical.

1600      Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake for heresy after refusing to abandon Pymander-based magic.

1650      Kircher creates his Qabalistic tree of life, which includes 22 paths that correspond to the Hebrew alphabet.

1736      Chosson of Marseille printed what would become the prototype for most later decks, known as the Chosson-Conver deck, in the Tarot de Marseille style.

1781      Antoine Court de Gébelin published his book Monde Primitif, which included tarot essays by himself and Le Comte de Mellet, and for the first time in print, the tarot trumps were associated with Hebrew alphabet letters.

The Pymander

The Major Arcana traditionally consists of 22 cards numbered from 0 to 21, though there is some evidence that it may also include the four aces, bringing the total to 26. They have traditionally been associated with spiritual matters.

Numbers such as [P6] in the following refer to numbered paragraphs within the Pymander.

Creation

The Demiurge

God creates the Demiurge [The Magician] who is responsible for the creation of the World in the Sphere of Fire/Creation [Wands], including the Governors and the Sensible world.

[P13]       God-the-mind, both male and female, brought forth another mind, the Demiurge (workman, craftsman) …

The Four Elements

The four aces represent the four elements that are birthed from the cloud [P6]. This cloud is plainly visible on the RWS Ace cards, with a hand emerging from it to produce the elemental symbols. Note that there are four higher elements created by God, and a matching four lower elements created by Nature.

[P6]       Then issues forth the Word of God. The Word descends from the light to the moisture (waters), and Fire is born from the female principle. The Fire flies upwards [Wands]. Air too rises from it [Ace of Swords], up to hang just below the Fire, leaving Earth and Water below.

[P7]       Earth [Pentacles] and Water [Ace of Ace of Cups] stayed so intermingled that they could not be distinguished from each other. Yet they were moved by the Word.

The Governors are created from the four higher elements, and are whirled around in circles. This section of the Pymander is typically depicted on The Wheel of Fortune card with the four types of animals in the corners, having wings and reading books to indicate their higher nature.

[P15]       The Demiurge, Mind, and Word combined with the workmanship of Nature, and the Demiurge contained the Governors and his workmanship, and set them to move in circles [The Wheel of Fortune], so that they have neither beginning nor end.

Plato’s Chariot

The meaning of several major cards – Chariot, Temperance and Justice – are not clearly identifiable in the Pymander, but instead are described in Plato’s Chariot Allegory, which in turn is found within Phaedrus. While this may seem like an inconsistency it can also be seen as confirmation that the Pymander, and the Major Arcana, are neo-platonic in nature. Interpreting these three cards according to the original Plato results in consistent interpretation with the Pymander

The full relevant text from Phaedrus, from English translation by Jowett, 1892, is given in Appendix C

Gods, Governors, and Secrets

In the following, the term Governor comes from the Pymander, and may be taken to mean the Roman Gods of the seven classical planets. It is interesting to note that the Pymander recognizes only the one God above all, and so refers to the planets as Governors rather than as Gods.

In the Major Arcana, the Roman Gods are rarely represented directly. This is likely deliberate to obscure the true meaning of the cards. This could be for either or both of two reasons. Firstly, these “secrets” were considered to be of great power, and not to be divulged to the masses. Secondly, anything of a pagan nature had to be kept from the eyes of the Catholic Church.

Oral tradition has it that the Major Arcana is a pictorial representation of a deep secret which had to be represented in pictures rather than written down, so that the secret could be passed using the cards, but accompanied by oral training. Given the coincidental timing of the emergence of the Pymander and the Tarot in Renaissance Italy, it seems likely that this secret is the Pymander itself. This book and matching deck assume that to be the case, and reconstruct the original meaning of the cards accordingly.

Historically, Pope Sixtus IV (1471 - 1484) accepted the Pymander as a valid addition to Christian lore, but his eventual successor Pope Clement VIII (1592 - 1605) reversed this decision in 1599, declaring it to be heretical.

Interpretation of Inverted cards

When a card appears inverted, consider its astrological influence. The card itself indicates the presence of the influence in the reading context, but we must consider that the querent may be either the source or the recipient of that influence.
In RWS, some cards appear to be negative when upright, and so positive when inverted. The reader may of course choose their own method of interpreting the reversal polarity of the card.

Also note that there may be different aspects to a decan, and often some aspects are not elaborated in the card imagery. Ultimately, the ideal is for the reader to be familiar with the full astrology of each card.

For the Pymander interpretations given, the interpretations focus on the direction of action of the influence.

Upright cards are considered to be the querent acting as a result of the decan’s influence, whereas inverted cards represent the querent being subjected to the influence via some other person or situation.
