The Court Cards

Introduction

The system presented here maps the Tarot court cards to Myers–Briggs personality types. Because MBTI describes a person’s inner cognitive style rather than their outward behaviour, the elemental correspondences derived from MBTI tend to align more closely with astrological Moon signs, which reflect instinctive perception and one’s private mode of understanding the world. This explains why certain individuals may not resemble the traditional Sun-sign archetype associated with a particular court card, yet match the MBTI-based elemental assignment with striking accuracy. For example, although Einstein’s Sun sign was Pisces, his Sagittarius Moon aligns precisely with the intuitive, big-picture cognition that defined his work, and matches the elemental qualities assigned to his MBTI type within this system.

To make practical use of this correspondence, the court cards are arranged as a 4×4 matrix, with one axis representing the four elemental suits and the other representing the four ranks. This structure mirrors the organisation of the MBTI itself: two of the MBTI axes determine the elemental suit, while the remaining two determine the rank. The result is a coherent framework in which each court card expresses a distinct cognitive style, elemental quality, and behavioural pattern. By grounding the Tarot in measurable population statistics and well-defined psychological polarities, the system restores clarity to the face cards and resolves many of the inconsistencies that have accumulated through tradition and oral transmission.

The sixteen face cards consist of the four ranks—King, Queen, Knight, and Page—expressed through the four elemental suits. While traditional Tarot assigns these roles through symbolic convention, the Pymander system derives them directly from the structure of the MBTI. Two MBTI axes determine the elemental suit, and the remaining two determine the rank, producing a complete and internally consistent 4×4 matrix. This approach not only clarifies the meaning of each court card but also reveals why certain cards appear more frequently in readings: the distribution of MBTI types in the population mirrors the relative rarity of Kings and Queens compared with the far more common Knights and Pages. By grounding the court cards in psychological typology and elemental theory, the system restores coherence to a part of the Tarot that has historically been fragmented by competing traditions.

To a small extent, the system used here follows Crowley’s scheme of a matrix of 16 cards, 4 x 4, with one axis being elemental suit, and another axis for rank of person portrayed. However, in this document and deck, Myers-Briggs type indicator is used, (which was itself an evolution of Jung’s work, which in turn drew on early categorisations which are consistent with the four Hermetic elements) with two of the indicators together indicating the four suits, and the other two indicating the four ranks

In order to better understand the meaning of the face cards, it is possible to map the face cards, by suit and rank, to various other systems:

● The Four Elements
● Playing cards
● Physical descriptions
● MBTI Personality descriptions
● DISC Personality Descriptions
● Psychology “Quotient” tests
● Astrological Moon Signs

The primary mapping described in this Pymander document is the MBTI, as it has population statistics which shed valuable light on some of the other mappings. Each will be discussed in turn.

Note that the analyses by each of these mappings seem to be generally consistent, although some issues and inconsistencies are noted. These seem to be the inevitable consequence of inevitable errors creeping-in over time in oral traditions, and also as a result in changes in metaphor over time.
